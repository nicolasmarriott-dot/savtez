CHECKER FINDINGS 2026-11-14: published measurements of one LLM checking another's output (LLM-as-judge, verifier/critic models, fact-checkers of generated text).
Scope: 15 findings from 15 distinct primary papers. Categories: (a) misses, (b) false alarms on correct output, (c) shared blind spots, (d) designs that cut false alarms without losing catches.
FACT lines are verbatim from the fetched PDF (pdftotext), with PDF page numbers. Table and figure values are quoted cell by cell and marked as such. Every number was re-located in the fetched text.
JUDGMENT lines are interpretation only, not source claims. Raw texts: source fetches (not in this release), named <arxiv-id>.{pdf,txt,plain.txt}

======================================================================
(a) HOW OFTEN THE CHECKER MISSES REAL ERRORS
======================================================================

F01
SOURCE: "LLMs cannot find reasoning errors, but can correct them given the error location", Gladys Tyen et al., arXiv 2023 (2311.08516). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.08516
FACT (p.4, Sec 3.1): "GPT-4 attains the best results but only reaches an overall accuracy of 52.87 with direct step-level prompting."
FACT (p.2): "We demonstrate that LLMs' low accuracy at few-shot mistake-finding can be improved upon without using in-domain training data."
JUDGMENT (interpretation): Even the strongest checker tested located the first wrong reasoning step only about half the time. A step-level checker that is used on its own should be expected to miss many errors.
CATEGORY: a
CAVEATS: BIG-Bench Mistake dataset (5 symbolic/arithmetic tasks, traces generated by PaLM 2). Few-shot prompting only. Models are 2023-era (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, PaLM 2, Gemini Pro). The metric is accuracy at locating the first mistake, not binary error detection. Table 9a (p.15) also shows high false-alarm rates on traces with no mistakes (e.g. Dyck languages, GPT-4 CoT step-level: 13.79% accuracy on correct traces).

F02
SOURCE: "Evaluating LLMs at Detecting Errors in LLM Responses" (ReaLMistake), Ryo Kamoi et al., COLM 2024 (2404.03602). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.03602
FACT (p.1, abstract): "Our findings show: 1) Top LLMs like GPT-4 and Claude 3 detect errors made by LLMs at very low recall, and all LLM-based error detectors perform much worse than humans."
FACT (p.8, Table 4, table values; responses from GPT-4-0613, task FgFactV = fine-grained fact verification): Recall: GPT-4 0613 detector 6.8, GPT-4 0125 detector 11.9, Expert Human 95.5. Precision: GPT-4 0613 detector 100.0, GPT-4 0125 detector 95.0. Caption: "Table 4: Error detection performance of 12 LLMs with zero-shot prompts on ReaLMistake. This table shows the average performance on four prompts in Section 4.3."
FACT (p.8, figure caption in Sec 4.1; extracted text has a space before the period): "Stronger LLMs detect errors made by GPT-4 with higher precision but with lower recall ."
JUDGMENT (interpretation): When GPT-4 checked GPT-4 output on fact verification, it caught only about 7-12% of real errors, though nearly every flag it raised was right. Strong checkers seem to fail by staying silent, not by raising false alarms. A clean pass from a strong single checker is therefore weak evidence that the output is correct.
CATEGORY: a (also b: precision)
CAVEATS: Three tasks, about 900 hand-annotated responses from GPT-4 and Llama 2 70B. Zero-shot binary prompts. Human performance was measured on 35 cases per setting. Results vary a lot by task (GPT-4 does much better on MathGen).

F03
SOURCE: "FELM: Benchmarking Factuality Evaluation of Large Language Models", Shiqi Chen et al., NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks Track (2310.00741). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.00741
FACT (p.8, Sec 4.2): "We observe that the majority of detectors performed unsatisfactorily on FELM, with only the GPT-4 evaluators achieving an overall average of F1 score greater than 40 in some settings."
FACT (p.1, abstract): "Our findings reveal that while retrieval aids factuality evaluation, current LLMs are far from satisfactory to faithfully detect factual errors."
JUDGMENT (interpretation): LLM fact-checkers of LLM text scored below 0.40 F1 at finding factual errors in most settings. Retrieval helps but does not solve the problem. A fact-checker without evidence documents should not be trusted alone.
CATEGORY: a
CAVEATS: ChatGPT responses across world knowledge, science, math, reasoning and writing. 2023-era evaluators (GPT-4, ChatGPT, Vicuna). F1 measured at segment level.

F04
SOURCE: "ProcessBench: Identifying Process Errors in Mathematical Reasoning", Chujie Zheng et al. (Qwen Team), arXiv 2024 (2412.06559; fetched version dated 2025-05-27). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.06559
FACT (p.5, Table 2, caption and table values): "“% Process errors” denotes the proportion of samples with erroneous reasoning steps (i.e., annotated as erroneous) among all the samples with correct final answers." Table values for "% Process errors (correct final answers)": GSM8K 3.5%, MATH 18.8%, OlympiadBench 32.2%, Omni-MATH 51.8%.
FACT (p.1, abstract): "(1) Existing PRMs typically fail to generalize to more challenging math problems beyond GSM8K and MATH. They underperform both critic models (i.e., prompted general language models) and our own trained PRM that is straightforwardly fine-tuned on the PRM800K dataset."
JUDGMENT (interpretation): A checker that only compares final answers would have passed solutions containing wrong steps in about half of the correct-answer Omni-MATH cases. On hard problems, a right answer does not prove the reasoning was right. Off-the-shelf PRMs did worse than prompted critic models here.
CATEGORY: a
CAVEATS: Math only. Solutions come from several open models and were labelled by human experts. Rates rise with problem difficulty.

======================================================================
(b) HOW OFTEN THE CHECKER FLAGS CORRECT OUTPUT
======================================================================

F05
SOURCE: "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet", Jie Huang et al., ICLR 2024 (2310.01798). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.01798
FACT (p.4, Table 3, table values; "Results of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 on reasoning benchmarks with intrinsic self-correction"): GPT-3.5 on CommonSenseQA: Standard Prompting 75.8, Self-Correct (round 1) 38.1, Self-Correct (round 2) 41.8. GPT-4 on GSM8K: Standard Prompting 95.5, Self-Correct (round 2) 89.0.
FACT (p.4): "Among the remaining instances, the model is more likely to modify a correct answer to an incorrect one than to revise an incorrect answer to a correct one."
JUDGMENT (interpretation): Without an external signal, an LLM reviewing its own work flagged and "fixed" correct answers often enough to roughly halve accuracy in one case. A checker's verdict should not be allowed to overwrite a carried fact unless it is grounded in evidence.
CATEGORY: b
CAVEATS: The checker reviews its own output (same model), not a different model. Prompt-based self-correction on GSM8K, CommonSenseQA and HotpotQA. 2023-era models.

F06
SOURCE: "Judging the Judges: Evaluating Alignment and Vulnerabilities in LLMs-as-Judges", Aman Singh Thakur et al., Proceedings of the Fourth Workshop on Generation, Evaluation and Metrics (GEM2 2025), ACL (2406.12624). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.12624
FACT (p.5): "Both false negatives and false positives increase as human alignment decreases, but well-aligned models tend to produce more false positives than false negatives."
FACT (p.5): "This is also visible in the general decline of alignment scores: while Llama-3 8B has a Scott’s π score of only 59, its percent agreement is still well above 80%."
FACT (p.1, abstract): "Further error analysis reveals vulnerabilities in judge models, such as sensitivity to prompt complexity and a bias toward leniency."
JUDGMENT (interpretation): Judges tend to err toward lenience. Raw percent agreement (over 80%) can hide weak chance-corrected agreement (Scott's pi 59). Report kappa or pi, not percent agreement, when validating a checker.
CATEGORY: b
CAVEATS: TriviaQA short answers with reference answers available. 13 judge models and 9 exam-taker models. The direction of a "false positive" (judge accepts a wrong exam answer) follows the paper's leniency framing. Check Sec 5.1 before relying on that reading.

F07
SOURCE: "LLM Critics Help Catch LLM Bugs" (CriticGPT), Nat McAleese et al., OpenAI, arXiv 2024 (2407.00215). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.00215
FACT (p.1, abstract): "On code containing naturally occurring LLM errors model-written critiques are preferred over human critiques in 63% of cases, and human evaluation finds that models catch more bugs than human contractors paid for code review."
FACT (p.1, abstract): "Critics can have limitations of their own, including hallucinated bugs that could mislead humans into making mistakes they might have otherwise avoided, but human-machine teams of critics and contractors catch similar numbers of bugs to LLM critics while hallucinating less than LLMs alone."
FACT (p.8, Sec 3.4): "Throughout the project we found that the probability of catching a bug increases with the number of claims that a critique makes."
JUDGMENT (interpretation): Catches and false alarms trade off directly. A critic that raises more claims catches more bugs and also invents more. The trade-off has to be set on purpose (here through FSBS length weighting, or by adding human review), not left to defaults.
CATEGORY: b (also d: FSBS and human+model teams)
CAVEATS: Code from OpenAI RLHF data, including bugs inserted by humans. Critic was RL-trained with proprietary data. Graded by contractors.

F08
SOURCE: "Large Language Models are not Fair Evaluators", Peiyi Wang et al., arXiv 2023 (2305.17926). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.17926
FACT (p.1, abstract): "This manipulation allows us to skew the evaluation result, making one model appear considerably superior to the other, e.g., Vicuna-13B could beat ChatGPT on 66 over 80 tested queries with ChatGPT as an evaluator."
FACT (p.1, abstract): "2) Balanced Position Calibration, which aggregates results across various orders to determine the final score"
JUDGMENT (interpretation): Changing only the order in which answers were shown was enough to flip a pairwise judge's verdict on most queries. Many of its "flags" reflected presentation order rather than content. Present answers in both orders and average the results.
CATEGORY: b (verdicts driven by order, not correctness)
CAVEATS: Vicuna benchmark, 80 questions, with ChatGPT and GPT-4 as evaluators. Pairwise preference judging, not error detection. I found no clean verbatim numeric sentence for the size of the calibration gain, so none is given.

(c) SHARED BLIND SPOTS / SELF-PREFERENCE / CORRELATED ERRORS
======================================================================

F09
SOURCE: "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena", Lianmin Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks Track (2306.05685). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.05685
FACT (p.5, "Self-enhancement bias" paragraph): "For example, GPT-4 favors itself with a 10% higher win rate; Claude-v1 favors itself with a 25% higher win rate."
FACT (p.5): "Due to limited data and small differences, our study cannot determine whether the models exhibit a self-enhancement bias."
FACT (p.1, abstract): "Our results reveal that strong LLM judges like GPT-4 can match both controlled and crowdsourced human preferences well, achieving over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans."
JUDGMENT (interpretation): Judges rated their own family's answers 10-25 points higher in win rate, while agreeing with humans over 80% of the time overall. Never let a model judge output from its own family.
CATEGORY: c
CAVEATS: The authors themselves call the self-preference evidence inconclusive. MT-Bench has 80 questions. 2023-era models.

F10
SOURCE: "Great Models Think Alike and this Undermines AI Oversight", Shashwat Goel et al., ICML 2025 (2502.04313). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.04313
FACT (p.5): "We find a significant (p < 0.01) positive correlation (average Pearson r=0.84) between LLM-as-ajudge scores and model similarity (κp ) for all judges."
FACT (p.1, abstract): "However, we observe a concerning trend – model mistakes are becoming more similar with increasing capabilities, pointing to risks from correlated failures."
FACT (p.5): "Across all judges we find a significant partial correlation, which implies that after controlling for accuracy there remains a relationship between judge score and model similarity."
JUDGMENT (interpretation): Judges score models that make the same mistakes they do more highly, even after accounting for accuracy, and these shared mistakes are increasing as models improve. Choose checkers for low error similarity (CAPA) to the model being checked, not only for strength.
CATEGORY: c
CAVEATS: Multiple-choice formats (MMLU-Pro and similar). CAPA is the authors' new metric. "ajudge" is a hyphenation artifact in the extracted text.

F11
SOURCE: "Correlated Errors in Large Language Models", Elliot Kim, Avi Garg, Kenny Peng, Nikhil Garg, ICML 2025 (2506.07962). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.07962
FACT (p.1): "For example, on Helm, pairs of models agree on average about 60% of the time when both models are incorrect (choosing between incorrect answers uniformly at random would lead to an agreement rate of 1/3)."
FACT (p.4): "each judge systematically inflates the accuracy of models that are less accurate than itself, due to correlated errors (the judge marks incorrect answers as correct if both models agree on the incorrect answer)."
FACT (p.4): "We find that models by the same developer, using the same base architecture, and having similar sizes are all associated with higher agreement"
JUDGMENT (interpretation): When two models are both wrong, they pick the same wrong answer about 60% of the time, against 33% by chance. A checker therefore approves exactly the errors it shares with the model it checks. Cross-checking within one family adds little independent coverage.
CATEGORY: c
CAVEATS: Multiple-choice leaderboards (HELM, Hugging Face). The judge was simulated by treating the judge's own answer as ground truth, not by free-form judging.

(d) CHECKER DESIGNS THAT CUT FALSE ALARMS WITHOUT LOSING CATCHES
======================================================================

F12
SOURCE: "Replacing Judges with Juries: Evaluating LLM Generations with a Panel of Diverse Models" (PoLL), Pat Verga et al., arXiv 2024 (2404.18796). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.18796
FACT (p.1, abstract): "Across three distinct judge settings and spanning six different datasets, we find that using a PoLL composed of a larger number of smaller models outperforms a single large judge, exhibits less intra-model bias due to its composition of disjoint model families, and does so while being over seven times less expensive."
FACT (p.4, Table 1, table values; "Cohen’s Kappa Judge Model Performance on Different Single-hop QA Datasets from KILT"), NQ / TQA / HPQA: GPT-4 0.627 / 0.841 / 0.830; PoLL 0.763 / 0.906 / 0.867; EM 0.651 / 0.827 / 0.662.
JUDGMENT (interpretation): A jury of three smaller models from different families (Command-R, Haiku, GPT-3.5) agreed with humans better than GPT-4 alone (kappa 0.763 vs 0.627 on NQ). Diversity across families did better than a single stronger judge.
CATEGORY: d
CAVEATS: QA and Chatbot Arena-style judging with reference answers available. The jury pools votes. Contrast F02: majority votes over three models did not improve error detection on ReaLMistake (Kamoi et al. p.20: "We do not observe improvement by taking the majority vote of predictions from multiple models."). Jury gains may depend on the task.

F13
SOURCE: "Long-form factuality in large language models" (SAFE), Jerry Wei et al., NeurIPS 2024 (2403.18802). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.18802
FACT (p.1, abstract): "Empirically, we demonstrate that LLM agents can outperform crowdsourced human annotators—on a set of ∼16k individual facts, SAFE agrees with crowdsourced human annotators 72% of the time, and on a random subset of 100 disagreement cases, SAFE wins 76% of the time."
FACT (p.5, Fig 4 caption): "On 16,011 individual facts, SAFE annotations agree with 72.0% of human annotations."
JUDGMENT (interpretation): Splitting a response into atomic facts and checking each against search results gave checker verdicts that beat crowd annotators in most disagreements. Retrieval grounding plus atomic claims is the strongest fact-check pattern measured here.
CATEGORY: d
CAVEATS: Biographies from FActScore data. The "wins" judgment comes from the authors' own annotation of 100 cases. Uses Google Search with GPT-3.5-Turbo.

F14
SOURCE: "Debating with More Persuasive LLMs Leads to More Truthful Answers", Akbir Khan et al., ICML 2024 (2402.06782). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.06782
FACT (p.1, abstract): "On the QuALITY comprehension task, we find that debate consistently helps both non-expert models and humans answer questions, achieving 76% and 88% accuracy respectively (naive baselines obtain 48% and 60%)."
FACT (p.2): "Specifically, for the most persuasive models we find that non-expert human judges achieve 88% and non-expert LLM judges achieve 76% accuracy with debate, where naive performance is 60% and 48% respectively."
JUDGMENT (interpretation): A weaker judge reading two expert models argue opposite sides reached 76% accuracy, against 48% when judging alone. Adversarial presentation can raise a checker's catch rate when the judge lacks the source text.
CATEGORY: d
CAVEATS: Reading comprehension with hidden passages and verified quotes (debaters' quotes are checked against the source). Two-choice questions. Gains depend on how persuasive the debaters are.

F15
SOURCE: "Let's Verify Step by Step", Hunter Lightman et al., OpenAI, arXiv 2023 (2305.20050). URL fetched: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.20050
FACT (p.2): "We use our state-of-the-art PRM to solve 78.2% of problems from a representative subset of the MATH test set."
FACT (p.7, Fig 3 legend, figure values, Best-of-1860): "ORM 72.4 PRM 78.2 Majority Voting 69.6"
JUDGMENT (interpretation): A verifier that scores each step (a process reward model) picked correct solutions more often than one scoring only the outcome (78.2% vs 72.4%) or majority vote (69.6%). Step-level checking beats whole-answer checking, but see F04 for its limits when moving to new domains.
CATEGORY: d
CAVEATS: Math only. The PRM was trained on 800K human step labels. Measured as best-of-N selection accuracy, not as a separate miss rate or false-alarm rate. ProcessBench (F04) found that existing PRMs generalize poorly to harder problems.
